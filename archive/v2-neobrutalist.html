A good guess isn’t the same as knowing why

The study measured whether a guess matched the next click. It never asked why anyone clicked. Your feed has to guess that part, too.

Where the numbers come from

For anyone who wants to check the main page, or doesn’t quite trust it yet.

The study

Four researchers published it in 2022 in the Information Retrieval Journal, a peer-reviewed journal, which means other experts checked the work before publication. They used reading records from three news websites. The main page uses one of them, an Italian-language site.

What they measured

For each click, a method guessed the one article the reader would open next. A guess counted only if it matched. “Clues from this visit” and “clues across visits” are plain names for what the study calls intra-session and inter-session information. “Both combined” weighs the two equally.

How to read the chart

11.40% means about 11 right guesses per 100. A news site has many articles, so one guess is hard to get right, and the study doesn’t say the methods are bad. Even the best one was guessing what people would click, not learning why.

What it can’t tell us

Whether readers liked the suggestions, why anyone clicked, or how any social media or video app works. It only checked guesses against the next click, on news websites.

Full citation: P. Symeonidis, D. Chaltsev, C. Berbague and M. Zanker, “Sequence-aware news recommendations by combining intra- with inter-session user information,” Information Retrieval Journal, vol. 25, pp. 461–480, 2022. Chart values from Table 5.

Read it at doi.org/10.1007/s10791-022-09415-w. The publisher may ask you to log in; many university libraries give students free access.

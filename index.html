So how often do those clues guess right?

A peer-reviewed study from 2022 tried both kinds of clues, and the two together, on the reading records of an Italian-language news site. For every click, each method made one guess at the next article the reader would open.

A good guess isn’t the same as knowing why

The study checked one thing: did the guess match the next click. It never asked anyone why they clicked. Your feed has to guess that part too, and it never finds out if it got that part wrong.

Why this matters for you

Your feed can’t tell a phase from a passion. All of these look the same from the outside:

three videos for one examshopping for someone else’s gifta week of breakup songs

So when something was only a phase, it helps to say so out loud. Many apps have a “Not interested” option tucked into the menu on a post, and it tells your feed something your clicks can’t.

Where the numbers come from

For anyone who wants to check the main page, or doesn’t quite trust it yet.

The study

Four researchers published it in 2022 in the Information Retrieval Journal, which is peer reviewed, so other experts checked the work before it ran. They used reading records from three news websites. The main page uses one of them, an Italian-language site.

What they measured

For each click, a method guessed the one article the reader would open next, and the guess only counted if it matched. “Clues from this visit” and “clues across visits” are plain names for what the study calls intra-session and inter-session information. “Both combined” weighs the two equally.

How to read the chart

11.40% works out to about 11 right guesses per 100. A news site holds a lot of articles, so landing one guess is genuinely hard, and the study doesn’t claim the methods are bad. Even the best one was predicting what people would click, not learning why.

What it can’t tell us

Whether readers liked the suggestions, why anyone clicked, or how any social media or video app works. It only checked guesses against the next click, on news websites.

Full citation: P. Symeonidis, D. Chaltsev, C. Berbague and M. Zanker, “Sequence-aware news recommendations by combining intra- with inter-session user information,” Information Retrieval Journal, vol. 25, pp. 461–480, 2022. Chart values from Table 5.

Read it at doi.org/10.1007/s10791-022-09415-w. The publisher may ask you to log in, and many university libraries give students free access.
